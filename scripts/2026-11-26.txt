Turning to world news.

In Yemen and Saudi Arabia, the fighting between the Houthis and the Saudi-led coalition widened Wednesday. The Houthis struck Saudi Arabia's Jizan port on the Red Sea. The coalition said the damage was minor, there were no civilian casualties, and that efforts are underway to determine the type of projectile. Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki said a Houthi drone was intercepted and destroyed over the port of Jeddah, and the coalition said it also intercepted Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh. That follows Tuesday's drone attack on a Riyadh skyscraper that injured at least 11 people. Yemen's government army spokesman said 654 strikes were carried out against the Houthis on Wednesday, and the Houthis said the coalition carried out 105 air and missile strikes on Sanaa and 10 provinces. Houthi media reported new Saudi strikes on the port of Hodeidah. Al Jazeera's correspondent in Sanaa noted that Hodeidah handles 90 percent of imported goods into Yemen.

In Ukraine, Russia fired ballistic missiles at Kyiv late Wednesday, hitting a shopping center and an industrial building on the city's east side. Euromaidan Press, citing local officials, reports seven people were injured. Overnight, a drone hit a high-rise in Kharkiv and killed a married couple, and four people were killed across the Kharkiv region in all. Ukraine's Air Force said Russia launched 113 attack drones along with several types of missiles, and that 87 drones were shot down or jammed. Separately, after meeting in Florida on Wednesday, President Trump and NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte issued a joint statement stressing, quote, the importance of Russia avoiding any actions that intentionally, or unintentionally, expand the war. Reuters reports they also discussed the war with Iran and defense spending.

Iran's Revolutionary Guard Navy said two oil tankers struck naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz on Wednesday, then exploded and caught fire, after using what it called an unauthorized route. The ships were not named, and no casualties were reported. An Iranian army spokesman said Iran has deployed new surveillance systems in the strait and warned that ships that pass without coordinating with Iranian forces face greater risk. Reuters reports Tehran says it will not restore normal shipping until the United States lifts its naval blockade of Iranian ports, and that Iran has offered, through Qatari mediators, to reopen the waterway within 48 hours if its conditions are met.

In Gaza, medics said Israeli airstrikes and gunfire killed at least nine Palestinians overnight, the third straight night of strikes despite the ceasefire that began in October of last year. One strike in Deir al-Balah killed Khaled Abu Nada, the Hamas-appointed deputy education minister, and his wife. Israel's military said Abu Nada ran Hamas's banking and investment network. Hamas confirmed his death but did not comment on Israel's claims about his role. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said, quote, no terrorist is immune, and no one in the Hamas leadership will manage to evade us.

North Korea's state media reports Kim Jong Un inspected nuclear material production and weapons research sites and called for, quote, utilising tactical nuclear weapons across multiple domains for different missions. Kim said North Korea's possession of nuclear weapons is, quote, an absolutely irreversible choice as long as malicious forces exist, and said any attempt to deny the country's nuclear status would be an act of war.

And in Egypt, 31 people were killed and 21 injured when a vehicle hauling fuel overturned and caught fire on a ring road in Greater Cairo, according to Reuters. About 27 vehicles were burned.

Now to news here at home.

A powerful nor'easter is hitting the East Coast this Thanksgiving, and for the first time in its history, the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade has been postponed because of weather. Organizers moved the 100th parade to Saturday, and NBC News reports it will begin at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. CBS reports concerns about wind and the giant balloons drove the decision. The parade was suspended during World War Two but had never been postponed for weather. In New Jersey, Governor Mikie Sherrill declared a state of emergency, warning of wind gusts up to 70 miles an hour, more than an inch of rain and coastal flooding. She urged residents to, quote, stay off the roads as much as possible once the storm arrives.

A second federal judge has blocked President Trump's newest executive order on birthright citizenship. The August order targeted what it called birth tourism, children of some foreign government and international organization workers, and children of parents classified as alien enemies. U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper in Washington issued a preliminary injunction Wednesday, writing that, quote, the President cannot carve out new exceptions to birthright citizenship. A judge in Maryland blocked the order in September. The White House, through spokeswoman Lauren Bis, called it, quote, just another ruling from an activist judge, and said the order is consistent with Supreme Court rulings. The American Civil Liberties Union, which brought the case, said, quote, third time is not the charm.

A federal judge has also barred the Federal Communications Commission from taking further steps that threaten ABC's broadcast licenses. Judge Loren Alikhan wrote, quote, this is not a close case, and said FCC Chair Brendan Carr, quote, has taken President Trump's rhetoric as his marching orders. Reuters did not report a response from the FCC, ABC or the White House.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention now reports nine measles-related deaths in the United States this year, though state-reported figures show at least 14. The CDC counts 6,039 confirmed cases in 47 jurisdictions, the most in 35 years, mostly among unvaccinated people. Reuters reports the gap between the federal and state death counts began after the CDC switched to a different mortality data source.

And in New York, a state judge upheld Mayor Zohran Mamdani's rent freeze on about 1 million rent-stabilized apartments, rejecting a challenge from landlords who called it a sham process. The ruling said, quote, elections have consequences. Mamdani called it a victory for tenants and affordability.

Now, Kentucky news.

Heavy rain is falling across the state on this Thanksgiving. The National Weather Service in Louisville expects a half inch to 3 inches of rain through Friday, with gusts of 25 to 30 miles an hour, and says the rain will help ease the ongoing drought. As of Wednesday afternoon, no flood watch had been issued. WKYT meteorologist Chris Bailey said he can't rule out local high-water issues where more than 2 inches falls, and that snow is possible at times in western and southwestern Kentucky, with light, slushy accumulations possible tonight, mainly on grass and elevated surfaces. AAA expects more than 1 million people on Kentucky roads for the holiday, and WKYT reports many drivers left Wednesday night to beat the rain.

The Kentucky Lantern reports counties across the state are facing what one official called a crisis in paying for local jails. Figures from the Kentucky Association of Counties show jail costs rose 76 percent from 2019 to 2025, to nearly 170 million dollars. The state pays counties about 35 dollars a day for each state inmate they house, while the association estimates the actual cost is about 63 dollars. Webster County Judge-Executive Steve Henry said his county spent about half its property tax revenue on its jail last year. Republican Representative Michael Meredith says he will refile a bill in January to require full reimbursement, which would cost about 200 million dollars a year. House budget chairman Jason Petrie, also a Republican, asked, quote, are we rehabbing the model, or are we supporting the model?

And the Lantern also reports Governor Andy Beshear's nonprofit political group, Heckbent Incorporated, raised 892,550 dollars from undisclosed donors in 2025, about 35 percent less than the year before, according to its federal tax filing. The group spent about 155,000 dollars on an unsuccessful push for a universal pre-K law. Beshear's separate super PAC has reported 10.7 million dollars in contributions this year.

Turning to AI news.

Nvidia's board has reinstated Jensen Huang as president and chief executive, weeks after removing him. In a federal filing, the board said it received input from senior U.S. government officials and is confident his return poses no national security risk. Huang said, quote, I take this responsibility with the greatest humility and renewed commitment. The board also created a national security and export control committee. The same day, the Commerce Department added Singapore-based Megaspeed and six affiliates to its trade blacklist, over suspicions it diverted Nvidia chips to China. A former Megaspeed executive, Alice Huang, is accused by U.S. officials of working with China's intelligence ministry, which China denies. Reuters reports U.S. officials remain divided, with the FBI assessing in recent briefings that the intelligence does not show she is an officer or asset of Chinese intelligence. Republican Senator John Curtis of Utah asked, quote, who in our government weighed in? Fox News reports Huang and Nvidia's general counsel are scheduled for interviews with House committees on December 1st and 2nd.

The Federal Communications Commission has approved SpaceX's plan for a network called Starmind, up to 1 million satellites that would act as data centers in orbit to run artificial intelligence computing. The FCC called it the first authorization in U.S. history for a constellation of data centers in space. SpaceX must have its first satellite operating by 2033 and the full network by 2040, and must file a plan to limit space debris. The FCC rejected objections from Amazon, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society, which said it was, quote, disheartened and dismayed. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said the decision opens, quote, a bold new frontier for computing infrastructure. Elon Musk said it will be a great asset to America and the world.

Axios, citing more than two dozen sources, reports that many employees at OpenAI and Anthropic believe some of their unreleased internal models are too powerful to release safely, and that staff are debating whether to keep top models internal-only. Anthropic told Axios that keeping a model internal does not by itself make it safe, and that it may slow or hold back releases even when that is costly. OpenAI said it has adopted new safety requirements that could slow releases. Separately, Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei posted Wednesday that he finds it absurd to suggest Anthropic wants people to hand control to AI. He wrote, quote, turning over governance to AI is a bad idea, and accused rivals of what he called vibes-based commercial and political positioning. Meta's chief AI officer, Alexandr Wang, had written that the world should not be okay with a, quote, handover of society to AI.

And for everyday users, TechCrunch reports OpenAI is combining the usage limits for ChatGPT's chat and work features, which means heavy users may hit their limits sooner.
