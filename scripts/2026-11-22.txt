Turning to world news.

NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte pledged Saturday in Warsaw to defend Poland, after two Russian missiles and a suspected jet-powered drone came down in southeastern Poland during Friday's large Russian attack on Ukraine. Officials believe one was a Kh-101 cruise missile. No injuries or damage were reported. Rutte said NATO will work out what happened and where its vulnerabilities lie. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk has called it an intentional provocation and said Warsaw will ask NATO to strengthen detection and surveillance over Poland. Al Jazeera reports President Trump described the missiles as landing, quote, by mistake. Poland has not invoked NATO's emergency consultation or collective defense clauses. NATO's governing council will discuss the incident Monday in Brussels.

In Ukraine, a Russian drone hit a five-story apartment building early Sunday in Dymer, in the Kyiv region. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said part of the building collapsed, and Reuters reports at least 14 people may be trapped under the rubble. Zelenskyy said nine people were evacuated and the fire was contained. Local officials said two people were hurt, including a teenager. On diplomacy, Zelenskyy said Saturday that the next round of talks among Ukraine, Europe, Russia and the United States, quote, depends on the American side. He said he hopes for talks before Christmas, and that Ukraine has not yet received an official invitation to next month's G20 summit in Miami, where President Trump has invited Vladimir Putin.

In the Baltic Sea, a drone attack Saturday morning set a sanctioned Russian cargo ship on fire in the Gulf of Finland. The ship, the Sparta Two, was bound for the Russian enclave of Kaliningrad. ABC Australia reports all 16 crew members were evacuated to a Russian vessel. A Finnish military spokesman said it is likely Ukraine was behind the attack, and Ukraine's Foreign Ministry did not respond to a request for comment. Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo wrote that the war is spilling over into Finland's immediate vicinity, and that Ukraine has the right to fight back against the aggressor.

At the United Nations climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, negotiators went into a second day of overtime. Early Sunday, summit President Murat Kurum said, quote, we believe that we have put forward a text which all the parties can agree upon. Reuters reports the draft would be the first official acknowledgment that warming is on course to pass 1.5 degrees Celsius, and it calls for faster development of technology to pull carbon out of the air. The draft does not name fossil fuels. It refers instead to an earlier agreement to transition away from them. Observers said Saudi Arabia and other oil producers pushed to keep fossil fuels out of the text, and a Saudi representative declined to comment. A final approval session was scheduled for early Sunday morning, and the outcome was not available at the time of this recording. The United States did not take part directly in this year's talks.

In Mexico, security forces captured Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, a cartel leader on the F B I's Ten Most Wanted list, with a five million dollar U.S. reward on his head. Mexico's Defense Ministry said seven alleged gunmen and four soldiers were killed in a firefight in Guasave, in Sinaloa state, that began Friday evening and lasted overnight. He was flown to Mexico City on Saturday. Mexico said U.S. intelligence helped, and the U.S. ambassador credited cooperation between the two countries. Mexican Security Secretary Omar Garcia Harfuch called it an important step in pacifying Sinaloa, and said troops will stay deployed there.

Now to news here in the U.S.

Wall Street closed out its worst week since March 2020. The Nasdaq fell 10 percent for the week, the S&P 500 lost 8.6 percent and fell all five days, and the Dow dropped 8 percent. The S&P 500 is now more than 10 percent below its October record, which is what traders call a correction. Chip stocks led the way down, with the semiconductor index off about 17 percent, its worst week since right after September 11th. The coming week is shortened by Thanksgiving, with markets closed Thursday. Investors will get new inflation and economic growth numbers.

President Trump signed an executive order Friday requiring federal contractors to notify the White House and their agency right away when Congress asks about their government work. They must follow administration instructions on withholding material the White House considers protected by executive privilege. Companies that don't comply could lose their contracts. Bloomberg Government estimates about 100,000 companies hold federal contracts. The order says Congress's ability to seek executive branch information is limited, and that poor coordination causes, quote, duplicative and wasteful efforts. Democrat Robert Garcia, the incoming chairman of the House Oversight Committee, said Congress has wide latitude to conduct oversight, and, quote, we are a co-equal branch of government and will not be deterred. Democrats take control of the House in January.

A judge in Missouri gave final approval Friday to Bayer's settlement over its Roundup weed killer. It could cost the company up to 7.25 billion dollars over the coming years. It covers people diagnosed now or in the future with non-Hodgkin lymphoma that they link to glyphosate, Roundup's main ingredient. Payments range from 10,000 to 165,000 dollars per person. The judge called it among the largest mass injury class action settlements in history. Bayer's CEO called it a decisive milestone, and a lawyer for the plaintiffs said it delivers guaranteed compensation. Bayer denies that Roundup causes cancer. The E P A has said it found no cancer risks of concern and is reviewing the evidence, while the World Health Organization's cancer agency classifies glyphosate as probably carcinogenic.

And storms on both coasts could slow Thanksgiving travel. An East Coast storm that started in Florida on Friday moves into the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast by this evening. A West Coast storm, called an atmospheric river, arrives Monday with 2 to 4 inches of rain forecast for northern California and southern Oregon through Wednesday. San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie asked residents to consider not traveling unless it is absolutely essential. A National Weather Service forecaster in Baltimore said major flooding or wind isn't expected there, but it doesn't take much to cause delays.

Now, A I news.

The fallout at Nvidia, the leading maker of A I chips, continues. Reuters reported Friday, citing two people briefed on the matter, that staff at the Securities and Exchange Commission are examining possible accounting issues at the company. The New York Times reported the inquiry is at an early stage and may not lead to charges. None of the outlets could say which accounting issues are under review. The SEC declined to comment, and Nvidia did not respond. Nvidia stock fell about 14 percent Friday to 125 dollars and 53 cents and lost 23 percent for the week. China denies the spying allegations, and Megaspeed says it operates lawfully. President Trump said Friday he would love to see Huang given a second chance, but board chair Harvey Jones said the board will not reinstate him.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC that President Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping agreed during their meeting in Shenzhen that humans should be involved in military decisions that use artificial intelligence. Bessent said both sides agreed, quote, no major military operation should be launched without a human being involved, and called it one of the major achievements of the Asia trip. Reuters notes the Commerce Department moved Monday to further restrict China's access to advanced A I chips, including through data centers overseas.

There's more debate over the claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the famous unsolved math problem about prime numbers. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpoge says he and Anthropic's Claude produced the proof, written only as code in Lean, a programming language that has a computer check every logical step. Alpoge says a human-readable version would run about 250,000 pages, and he acknowledges he does not fully understand all of it. M I T's Andrew Sutherland independently compiled the code and said the chance of error was approximately zero, though a second check, confirming the code proves the hypothesis exactly as stated, was still pending. Oxford's James Maynard told the New York Times he was 98 or 99 percent certain. Stanford's Brian Conrad called releasing a proof its author doesn't fully understand, quote, frankly completely outrageous.

And in consumer privacy, the messaging app Signal says it will block screenshots by default on the next version of the iPhone software, and on Android whenever an A I assistant tries to capture the screen. Signal says the goal is to stop phone assistants such as Apple's Siri and Google's Gemini from reading private chats, and it calls A I agents built into phones an existential threat. Agents are assistants that can take actions for a user, including reading what's on screen. Signal says the protection has limits: if the other person in a chat turns it off, their assistant can still read the messages.

And in science, researchers led by the University of St Andrews in Scotland flew a small drone through the spouts of humpback whales off British Columbia, catching their breath in sterile dishes. Next, the team wants to learn whether whales that feed together are, in the lead researcher's words, family, or long-term friends.
